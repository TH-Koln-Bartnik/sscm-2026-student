Quality is also a flow problem

Chapter 6 and Supplement 6 look at quality from the customer’s side: is the product within spec? Factory physics looks at the same defects from the line’s side: what do they do to output and waiting time (Heizer et al., 2023; Hopp & Spearman, 2011)? Seven ideas connect the two views.

  1. One source, two damages. A noisy process makes strips out of spec (Heizer: scrap, cost of quality) and irregular step times, so the next station waits (factory physics: queues). Reducing variation fixes both.
  2. Rework hits a station twice. Every reworked unit passes the station again, so the station is busy more of the hour. And some units take one pass, others two, so the step times get more irregular. Both make the queue longer.
  3. Near full load, a little rework more than doubles the waiting. Waiting grows with u ÷ (1 − u), where u is the share of the hour a station is busy. At 85 % busy the factor is 0.85 ÷ 0.15 ≈ 5.7. Ten percent rework raises the load to 93.5 %, and the factor jumps to 0.935 ÷ 0.065 ≈ 14.4: about 2.5 times the waiting from 10 % more work.
  4. Inspect before the bottleneck. Heizer asks where to inspect. Factory physics adds the rule that decides: a defect caught before the bottleneck has cost cheap time; a defect caught after it has used time on the one station that sets the whole line’s output, and that time is gone.
  5. Scrap means starting more than you sell. Effective output = nominal output × yield. A line that can make 54 units a day and rejects 1 in 12 delivers 54 × 11/12 = 49.5 good units. To deliver 54, it must start 54 ÷ (11/12) ≈ 59.
  6. Small WIP finds defects fast. When a station starts making defects, every unit already waiting between it and the inspection point passes before the first bad one is seen. With 20 units in between, about 20 bad units are made; with 4, about 4. Low work in process and good quality support each other.
  7. In control is not the same as good enough. A control chart tells you the process is stable; Cp and Cpk tell you whether it is capable. Example: someone cuts 10-cm paper strips by eye, and samples of 5 show R̄ = 1.0 cm, so σ ≈ R̄ ÷ d₂ = 1.0 ÷ 2.326 ≈ 0.43 cm. Against a spec of 10.0 ± 0.5 cm, Cp = 1.0 ÷ (6 × 0.43) ≈ 0.39. Every chart point can sit inside the limits while a fixed share of strips misses the spec. That share is the lost yield of idea 5. The fix is a better process (a ruler), not closer watching.

Test 2 preparation

What Test 2 asks (written this morning)

When and how. Thursday, 22 October, 09:00. First alone (iRAT), then the same questions in your team (gRAT). On paper, calculator allowed, phones and books closed; your code name on the answer sheet; write the calculation path in the boxes.

What Test 2 asks: calculations. Four kinds, as practised in class and on the bonus sheets, with new numbers:

  1. Crossover charts. Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × volume, one line per option; the crossover is where two lines cross: difference in fixed cost ÷ difference in variable cost. You use it twice:
    • Process types (Ch. 7): which process (process focus, repetitive, product focus) is cheapest at which volume; break-even volume = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost per unit)
    • Vehicles and equipment (S5): the crossover in km, and in years = km ÷ km per year
  2. Life cycle cost (S5, Equation S5-2): purchase price + energy cost per km × km per year × years + other operating cost per km × km per year × years; which option is cheaper over its life
  3. Carbon footprint: Σ amount × emission factor, stage by stage, with the amount in the factor’s unit (kg, kWh, tonne-km); the share of the biggest stage; the hotspot
  4. Process efficiency and capacity (Ch. 7 + S7), as on the Week 2 bonus sheet: design and effective capacity; utilization = actual ÷ design; efficiency = actual ÷ effective; capacity of each step, bottleneck, line output, throughput time; people or machines needed (round up); the bottleneck of a supply chain

You should be able to draw a crossover chart by hand and read the cheapest option for a given volume or distance from it.

The Backhaus Kaya case is test content. The calculations of both parts of the case are part of Test 2, with new numbers:

  • Part 1 (Week 2): the oven’s design and effective capacity, utilization and efficiency; the bottleneck of the night shift; the crossover of the current process and the roll line, and the break-even volume of the contract; the capacity of the chain flour mill → bakery → van
  • Part 2, “The carbon clause” (Week 3): the footprint of one roll by stage and its hotspot; the life cycle cost and crossover of the two vans; round trips and crates (round up); cost per tonne avoided and the cheapest-first plan for the contract target
