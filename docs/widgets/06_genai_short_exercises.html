A · CEBMa levels of evidence

The guideline ranks a study by how well its design can show cause and effect (“if we do A, does B follow?”): a control group, random assignment and a before-and-after measurement make the strongest single study, and a systematic review that pools such studies ranks highest.

LevelDesign
AAsystematic review or meta-analysis of randomized controlled studies
Asystematic review or meta-analysis of non-randomized controlled and/or before-after studies; randomized controlled study
Bsystematic review or meta-analysis of controlled studies without a pretest or uncontrolled studies with a pretest; non-randomized controlled before-after study; interrupted time series
Csystematic review or meta-analysis of cross-sectional studies; controlled study without a pretest or uncontrolled study with a pretest
Dcross-sectional study (survey)
Ecase studies, case reports, traditional literature reviews, theoretical papers

Two points an AI often skips. The ladder is for cause-and-effect questions; for “how many, how often?” a survey can be the most appropriate design. And the CAT guideline gives letters, not percentages: if your AI gave percentages, ask it where they come from.

B · The Duhem–Quine thesis

A claim is never tested alone. A prediction follows only from the claim plus auxiliary assumptions: the instrument works, the data are clean, the background theory holds, the conditions were as assumed. If the prediction fails, logic says only that something in this bundle is wrong, not what. Duhem showed this for physics (1906); Quine extended it to all of our knowledge (1951). The thesis does not say that tests are useless; it says that one failed (or passed) test does not settle a single claim until you have looked at the assumptions it rested on.

Six starter claims already in the tree

for the thesis

Data-centre electricity grows far faster than the grid.

Global data-centre demand rose about 17 % in 2025, AI-focused data centres about 50 %; the IEA expects about 950 TWh by 2030, roughly 3 % of world electricity.

for the thesis

Big models and video cost far more than a short text prompt.

One answer of a large open model took about 1.9 Wh including cooling; a five-second AI video about 0.94 kWh. Data-centre electricity in the US was about 48 % more carbon-intensive than the US average.

for the thesis

AI's water use is hidden, and it bites locally.

Global AI demand may withdraw 4.2–6.6 billion m³ of water in 2027 and evaporate (consume) 0.38–0.60 billion m³; where and when a model runs changes its water use several-fold.

Counts: world, per year, estimate

Li, P., Yang, J., Islam, M. A., & Ren, S. (2025). Making AI less “thirsty”. Communications of the ACM, 68(7), 54–61. https://doi.org/10.1145/3724499

against the thesis

A median text prompt is tiny, and it keeps shrinking.

Google measured 0.24 Wh of energy and 0.26 mL of water for the median Gemini text prompt, and a 33-fold drop in energy per prompt within one year.

Counts: per prompt, measured by the provider

Elsworth, C., Huang, K., Patterson, D., et al. (2025). Measuring the environmental impact of delivering AI at Google scale (arXiv:2508.15734). https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2508.15734

against the thesis

Ten prompts a day barely move your personal footprint.

At 0.3–3 Wh per query, ten queries a day are about 0.02–0.2 % of a UK resident's daily electricity, and at most about 11 kg CO₂ a year; diet, heating, driving and flights matter far more.

Counts: per person, per year, estimate

Ritchie, H. (2025, May 6). What's the carbon footprint of using ChatGPT? By the Numbers [Substack]. https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt

against the thesis

Data-centre water is small next to other uses.

Counted generously, with the water at power plants, a prompt uses about 2 mL; US data centres' on-site water use is a few per cent of what US golf courses use.
