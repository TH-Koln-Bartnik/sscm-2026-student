Appendix F — OM pioneers

The people behind the code names

Your code name belongs to a real person who changed how work is organised, measured or planned. Each sketch says who they were, what they left us and where it turns up in this module. Follow the links for the full story.

The sketches are short AI-assisted summaries for a quick overview. Check important details against the linked sources.

Work and productivity

Taylor1856 — Frederick W. Taylor (1856–1915). As an engineer at Midvale Steel, Taylor timed each step of a job with a stopwatch and rebuilt the job around the fastest method. The Principles of Scientific Management (1911) made this approach famous around the world. Critics then and since have objected that it treats workers like machine parts. Week 1 starts from his question: output per hour of what? (ASME)

Gilbreth1878 — Lillian Moller Gilbreth (1878–1972). One-page handout (PDF). An engineer and psychologist, she ran motion studies with her husband Frank and studied how fatigue wears down performance. Later she interviewed thousands of women to set the right heights for stoves and sinks. She raised twelve children, the family behind Cheaper by the Dozen. Her lesson for Week 1 is that productivity begins with the person doing the work. (ASME)

Gantt1861 — Henry Gantt (1861–1919). Gantt worked with Taylor and then went his own way. He designed a task-and-bonus wage system and drew the bar chart that compares planned work with work actually done. Your group project will put that chart to use. (OpenStax)

Follett1868 — Mary Parker Follett (1868–1933). A social worker turned management thinker, Follett argued for “power with” people rather than “power over” them. She held that conflict is best settled by integration, a solution that serves both sides, not by one side winning. Management writers, Peter Drucker among them, later rediscovered her work. She is the patron of your team work. (London Business School; Wikipedia)

Woodward1916 — Joan Woodward (1916–1971). A British sociologist, Woodward studied manufacturing firms in South Essex and sorted them by how they produce: unit and small batch, large batch and mass, continuous process. The firms that did well had the organisation their technology called for, a founding result of contingency theory (Industrial Organization: Theory and Practice, 1965). In 1969 she became Professor of Industrial Sociology at Imperial College, the second woman to hold a chair there. Week 2’s process types ask her question. (Wikipedia)

Flow and production systems

Ford1863 — Henry Ford (1863–1947). Ford’s Highland Park plant had a moving assembly line by 1913. It made the Model T cheap enough for its own workers, whose pay he doubled to five dollars a day. His legacy has a dark side: his newspaper spread antisemitic conspiracy lies in the 1920s. The process choices in Week 2 begin with his line. (The Henry Ford; USHMM)

Toyoda1894 — Kiichiro Toyoda (1894–1952). The son of the loom inventor Sakichi Toyoda, Kiichiro steered the family firm into cars; Toyota calls him the founder of Toyota Motor Corporation (established 1937). He proposed “just-in-time” production: make only what is needed, when it is needed. Week 6 follows that idea along the supply chain. (Toyota)

Ohno1912 — Taiichi Ohno (1912–1990). A Toyota engineer, Ohno built the Toyota Production System on Kiichiro’s idea and named the seven wastes (muda) that every lean team still hunts. Look for his thinking wherever this module asks what in a process adds no value. (Wikipedia; Toyota)

Goldratt1947 — Eliyahu M. Goldratt (1947–2011). An Israeli physicist, Goldratt wrote The Goal (1984), a business novel about a failing plant. It made the Theory of Constraints famous: a system runs only as fast as its bottleneck, so manage the bottleneck first (drum-buffer-rope). Week 2’s capacity analysis is his ground. (TOC Institute)

Little1928 — John D. C. Little (1928–2024). An MIT professor, Little published the proof of L = λW in 1961. Items in a system equal arrival rate times time spent inside, and it holds for queues, warehouses and inboxes alike. He was the first president of INFORMS, the professional society for operations research. You will use his law in Week 2. (INFORMS)

Shingo1909 — Shigeo Shingo (1909–1990). An industrial engineer and consultant, Shingo taught Toyota’s supervisors and engineers from about 1955 to 1980. He coined “single-minute exchange of die” (SMED): split a changeover into the work that needs the machine stopped and the work that can be done while it runs, then move as much as possible into the second group. His poka-yoke devices make a mistake hard to make in the first place, so quality is built in rather than inspected later, a Week 4 idea. Utah State University’s Shingo Prize (1988) carries his name. (Lean Enterprise Institute; Wikipedia)

Forrester1918 — Jay W. Forrester (1918–2016). At MIT, Forrester led the Whirlwind computer project and patented magnetic core memory (1949), an early form of RAM. He then turned to management: the boom-and-bust cycle of a General Electric appliance plant led him to found system dynamics in the mid-1950s (Industrial Dynamics, 1961). His “refrigerator game” became the Beer Game, which shows how delays and feedback make orders swing along a supply chain. Week 6 meets that swing. (MIT News)

Quality and statistics

Nightingale1820 — Florence Nightingale (1820–1910). At the military hospital in Scutari during the Crimean War, Nightingale saw more soldiers die of disease than of wounds. She proved it with her polar area chart and used the evidence to change how hospitals are run. She was the first woman elected to the Statistical Society (1858; some sources say 1859). In Week 4 she shows that a good chart can win an argument. (Florence Nightingale Museum; University of York)

Newbold1882 — Ethel May Newbold (1882–1933). Newbold came to statistics through war work at the Ministry of Munitions and went on to analyse industrial accidents for the Medical Research Council. Her work with Major Greenwood on repeated accidents made her, in 1928, the first woman to receive the Royal Statistical Society’s Guy Medal. Her accident data are an early form of safety analytics. (Wikipedia; IMS)

Shewhart1891 — Walter A. Shewhart (1891–1967). At Bell Telephone Laboratories, Shewhart sketched the first control chart in a one-page memo dated 16 May 1924. He taught engineers to tell ordinary variation from a real signal. The improvement cycle that Deming spread began with him, and Week 4 rests on both ideas. (Wikipedia)

Deming1900 — W. Edwards Deming (1900–1993). From 1950, Deming taught statistical quality control to Japanese managers and engineers, and Japan’s top quality award, the Deming Prize, carries his name. His 14 Points and the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle put the blame for most defects on the system, not on the worker. (The W. Edwards Deming Institute)

Juran1904 — Joseph M. Juran (1904–2008). Juran edited the Quality Control Handbook (1951) and coined “the vital few and the trivial many”, naming the idea after Vilfredo Pareto. He lectured in Japan from 1954 and framed quality management as a trilogy of planning, control and improvement. Every Pareto chart in Week 4 is his. (Wikipedia)

Hicks1919 — Beatrice A. Hicks (1919–1979). A chemical engineer, Hicks became chief engineer and vice president of Newark Controls, where she designed pressure and gas density controls for aircraft and missiles and invented a gas density switch. She was the first president of the Society of Women Engineers, founded in 1950, and in 1965 the first woman to receive a doctorate in engineering from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. Her controls are measuring instruments that keep a process inside its limits, the Week 4 theme. (Society of Women Engineers; Wikipedia)

Optimisation and planning

Kantorovich1912 — Leonid Kantorovich (1912–1986). In 1938, a Leningrad plywood trust asked this Soviet mathematician how to share work across its machines for the most output. His 1939 booklet on production planning held the core ideas of linear programming. He shared the 1975 Nobel Prize in economics with Tjalling Koopmans. (Nobel Prize)

Dantzig1914 — George B. Dantzig (1914–2005). As a student, Dantzig came late to class and copied two famous unsolved statistics problems from the board, thinking they were homework, and solved them. Working on planning for the US Air Force, he developed the simplex method (1947), the engine behind linear programming. Week 7’s planning models stand on it. (INFORMS)

Land1927 — Ailsa Land (1927–2021). At the London School of Economics, Land and Alison Doig published branch and bound in 1960. The method still drives the solvers that pick whole-number answers: three trucks, not 2.7. In 1980 she became the first woman in Britain to hold a full professorship in operational research. (LSE)

Harcourt1929 — Alison Harcourt, née Doig (born 1929). The other half of the Land–Doig method returned to Melbourne and helped Ronald Henderson’s team measure poverty in the 1960s, research behind Australia’s Henderson poverty line. She went on to teach statistics at the University of Melbourne. Her path shows the same optimisation tools at work in social policy. (Graduate Women Victoria; Wikipedia)
